# Project Brief (plain English)

For the group. No jargon. The detailed versions are in [literature.md](literature.md) and [features.md](features.md).

## The problem in one paragraph

Someone calls your bank pretending to be you, using a cloned voice. We want a model that listens to the call and says "this voice is synthetic." The hard part is not detecting fake voices in general. That is mostly solved on clean studio audio. The hard part is doing it on a **phone line**, in **real time**.

## Where the fake gives itself away

Fake speech is generated by a **vocoder**, the component that turns numbers into an actual sound wave. Vocoders are not perfect, and they leave fingerprints:

- **High-frequency detail is wrong.** Synthetic audio has odd or missing energy in the upper part of the spectrum. This is the single strongest tell in the literature.
- **Noisy sounds are done badly.** Consonants like s, f, sh, and breath sounds are noise, not tones. Vocoders are bad at noise.
- **Pitch is too smooth.** Real human pitch wobbles slightly all the time. Synthetic pitch is often too clean.
- **Breathing and mouth sounds are missing.** Many TTS systems just skip them.

One tell we must **not** use: the silence at the start and end of a recording. Models love it, because in the standard dataset fake clips have different silence than real ones. It works in the lab and is worthless in the field. An attacker deletes the silence, and a live call has no clean silence anyway.

## Why the phone line is the hard part

This is the core tension of the project, so it is worth stating clearly:

**The fingerprint lives in the high frequencies. The phone line deletes the high frequencies.**

A normal phone call keeps roughly 300 Hz to 3400 Hz. Everything above that is gone, not damaged, gone. So the strongest evidence we have is exactly the evidence the channel throws away.

It gets worse in two ways:

1. **The phone codec is itself a vocoder.** GSM compresses speech using the same kind of technique that generates fake speech. So a real human voice, after going through GSM, starts to look synthetic. That means false alarms on real customers, not just missed attacks.
2. **Compression hides the hardest attacks the most.** The drop in accuracy is not spread evenly. The attacks that were already difficult become much more difficult.

## The biggest problem is not the phone line

This reframes the project. Models trained on the standard dataset collapse when tested on **any other** dataset, even with no compression at all. One published model scores 1.2% error in-domain and 37% error on real-world audio. Training it on more of the same dataset changes nothing, because it is all the same source recordings.

So: **generalization across datasets is a bigger problem than the phone codec.** We have to design the evaluation around that, or we will fool ourselves.

## How we proceed

1. **Get a baseline working.** Take an existing model (RawNet2 is the sensible starting point), train on the standard dataset, reproduce known numbers. This just proves our setup is correct.
2. **Break it on purpose.** Push the test audio through real phone codecs and measure how much accuracy we lose. We know roughly what to expect, so we will know if our setup is wrong.
3. **Test on a second, different dataset.** Measure the bigger collapse. Report the two numbers separately, never pooled.
4. **Try the fixes that have evidence behind them.** Train with codec augmentation. Use the right input features (anything except mel). Prefer models that work on the raw waveform.
5. **Make it streaming.** Instead of one decision per recording, make a decision every few seconds. Measure the latency and how much accuracy it costs. Nobody in this literature has measured this, which is why it is our contribution.
6. **Write it up honestly.** The conclusion will almost certainly be that this is a **risk signal**, not a gate. It gets combined with other checks. It does not authenticate anyone by itself.

## What we measure

- Error rate on clean audio (the reference point)
- Error rate per phone codec (how much the channel costs)
- Error rate on a different dataset (how much generalization costs)
- **False alarms on real human voices sent through GSM** (nobody has measured this, and it is cheap for us to do)
- Milliseconds per decision, and accuracy at each window length

## One piece of good news

The literature says "use the full recording, do not cut it into short chunks." That would kill real-time. But the actual numbers on realistic audio show short 4-second chunks performing **better** than full recordings for the best model. The advice appears to be an artifact of the clean dataset. So real-time may cost us far less than expected, and that is directly testable in step 5.
